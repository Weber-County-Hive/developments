Pleasant Plains: Up to 3,500 Homes Proposed in Plain City

A draft agreement would allow up to 3,500 homes and 300 acres of commercial and industrial use on about 1,558 acres. The developer has set a state-law clock: the City Council must approve or deny it by Monday, Nov. 23, 2026. The public hearing is Thursday, Nov. 12, at 7 p.m.

The short version

  • Pleasant Plains is a proposed master development agreement between Plain City and Cole West Land Partners, on land owned by the Jackson family and Pleasant Plains Ranch, Inc.
  • The Oct. 2, 2026 draft allows up to 3,500 homes and up to 300 acres of commercial uses, "including, without limitation, retail, office, industrial, hospitality, and entertainment."
  • On Oct. 7 the owners and developer served a letter under Utah Code 10-20-905 requiring the City Council to approve or deny by Nov. 23, 2026.
  • State law and Plain City's own code both let the council say no. If it denies, it must give its reasons in writing.
  • The public hearing is Thursday, Nov. 12, at 7 p.m. at City Hall.

What's proposed

The legal description in the draft covers three parcels totaling about 1,558 acres (1,500.5, 57.9 and 0.35 acres), bounded in places by 3900 West, 4200 West, the Little Mountain Rail Trail and First and Second Salt Creek.

What Cole West told the water district

At the Bona Vista Water Improvement District board meeting on Aug. 31, 2026, according to the minutes:

  • Cole West said "it has more than 3,000 acre-feet of water rights available for consideration."
  • Cole West suggested "a dedication rate of approximately 0.25 might be more appropriate than the current 0.34 rate." District staff member Greg Seegmiller "disagreed with certain aspects of ColeWest's analysis."
  • The district's legal counsel said "the proposal was premature for the Board to make a determination." The board took no vote and asked Cole West to submit a proposal.
The Oct. 7 letter says the developer will deliver a "signature-ready" agreement at least five business days before the meeting where the council takes final action. That version may differ from the Oct. 2 draft described here.

How the decision works

  1. 1March 31, 2026The owners and developer petition Plain City for the agreement and pay a $20,000 fee.
  2. 2Oct. 7, 2026They serve a letter under Utah Code 10-20-905 starting a 45-day clock and asking for a written decision.
  3. 3Thursday, Nov. 12, 7 p.m.Public hearing at City Hall. Residents can speak.
  4. 4By Monday, Nov. 23The City Council must approve or deny.

What can happen next

ApproveThe agreement becomes land use law for the property for up to 30 years.
DenyThe council states its reasons in writing and on the record, which can be the minutes.
No actionThe developer can ask a district court to step in within 30 days. The law allows no money damages under this section.

Can the city say no?

Yes, the records support that.

  • The state law the developer invoked says the council "shall take final action by approving or denying the petition" (Utah Code 10-20-905(2)(c)(ii)).
  • Approving a development agreement like this one is a legislative act. Courts presume these decisions are valid and review them only for whether they are "reasonably debatable" (10-20-1109).
  • Plain City's code says the city may reject a proposal "at any time before approval and for any reason" (City Code 11-9-4(A)).
  • Plain City's code lists the purpose of these agreements: projects that align with the General Plan, improve more than 100 acres, and offer "extreme benefits" to the city (11-9-1).
If the council denies, the developer has asked for a written decision, so the reasons must be stated in writing and on the record (10-20-905(2)(d)). This page is a summary of public law, not legal advice. The city attorney should confirm.

If the city says no, can the land go to the county?

Not directly. Weber County's zoning applies only to unincorporated land (Utah Code 17-79-101). To fall under county rules, the land would first have to leave Plain City through disconnection (Utah Code 10-2-501 to 510):

  1. Owners of more than 50% of the private land in the area petition.
  2. The Plain City Council holds a public hearing and decides.
  3. If the council says no, the petitioner can go to district court and must show, among other things, that "justice and equity require" it.
  4. It takes effect only when the lieutenant governor issues a certificate.

No 2024–2026 state law was found that lets the state overrule a city's denial of an agreement like this one.

PID vs. IFD

The draft does not mention an Infrastructure Financing District (IFD). It says the developer "may explore" a Public Infrastructure District (PID), Special Improvement District or Special Assessment Area, each needing a separate council vote (section 5.06).

Questions about the draft

These questions have been raised about the agreement. They are open until the developer or the city answers them in public.

Phasing and roads (4.03, 7.01)If the developer can set the size and order of phases without city approval, how does the city keep building in step with roads and ways out of the area?
Density (4.04)The four higher-density caps add up to 2,800 homes. The Oct. 2 draft adds a 45% cap on stacked and attached homes (1,575). Should there also be minimums for the lower-density levels, and does this mix fit the General Plan?
The corridor road (4.06)If UDOT hasn't set the West Weber Corridor in five years, a collector road gets built across the property. Where would it connect, and should commercial and high-density building wait for the corridor?
Wetlands (5.03)If land turns out to be wetlands and can't be built on, why shouldn't the number of homes go down?
Density per acre (5.08)3,500 homes on about 1,558 acres is about 2.2 homes per acre overall. Does any per-acre figure the developer has given count parks, commercial land and unbuildable land?
Water (5.01(a))Cole West told the Bona Vista water board it has more than 3,000 acre-feet of water rights "available for consideration." Are those shares of the type that can be used for homes and businesses, and what does the agreement require them to prove before building?
Sewer (5.01(b))Does the 17.5-acre sewer site connect to Plain City's existing treatment plant? What is the plant's capacity, who pays to expand it, and would a PID be part of that?
Existing roads (5.01(c), 5.04)Who pays to widen the roads residents already use to get in and out of the city?
Accessory dwellings (4.09)The draft allows detached ADUs on lots of 8,000 square feet or more, and ADUs "shall not count toward the Maximum Residential Density." How does that compare with the city's current lot-size rules, and how many more homes could that add?
Setbacks and utilities (4.10, 5.01(e))Are 12-foot front setbacks and the garage setback (still blank) enough for parked trucks, sidewalks and 10-foot utility easements? The draft names Dominion Energy as the gas provider; has that been updated?
Parks and "extreme benefits" (5.02)Two parks and a rec center site add up to about 25 acres, under 2% of the land. City code calls for "extreme benefits." What are they?
PID (5.06)Should the PID option stay in the agreement at all, and who would carry that debt?
Impact fee credits (5.06)If land is dedicated to another public, religious or charitable group, the city would owe the developer equal value. Is that normal, and what could it cost?
Renewal (12.01)Should the agreement renew automatically, or should each renewal come back to the council?
